## HAM7 Day

One of my favorite days this summer was the chance to work in the laser vacuum and equipment area (LVEA) and getting hands-on with HAM7 (Horizontal Access Module 7). HAM7 houses the quantum squeezer and its crystal, which converts one higher-energy photon into two lower-energy photons that produce squeezed light. We are able to get more sensitivity at higher frequencies using this technique. You can read more about the quantum squeezer (here)[https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/news/ligo20231023]. I got into full-bunny clean room garb to assist staff scientist Camilla Compton with aligning some laser beams. We used laser alignment cards and my iPhone camera to see the near infrared laser with our own eyes. All GW data and analyses rely on every part of the detector working in tandem, perfectly. I only spent a couple of hours adjusting the quantum squeezer paths which was a lot of hard work in lots of layers of garb. I am so grateful for all of the scientists that work during upgrades and during observing runs to maintain a working interferometer.

## GWANW: Hosting the GW Explorer Tutorial

A highlight of the last few weeks was the Gravitational Wave Astrophysics in the Northwest (GWANW) meeting, held right here at LIGO Hanford Observatory. I presented my home research on Early Warning GWs and hosted a tutorial built around my GW Explorer project (read about the project: (GW Explorer Workshop at Las Vegas Academy))[https://rachellanggin.com/blog/2025/GWExplorer/]. This was a fantastic opportunity to have GW researchers use the tutorials. I got immense positive feedback from the participants and I'm even more excited to see this project grow beyond the Las Vegas bubble that it was constructed in. I even had lots of interest in new contributors to the project and desire to translate the project into more languages!

## A Saturday Tour with Fred Raab

I also tagged along on one of the First Saturday Public Tours, led by Fred Raab, who's been with LIGO since the very beginning. Getting the history from someone who was actually there isn't something I take for granted, so I showed up on a Saturday for this one. Fred told us about the installation and what's been learned after 20 years of running. My favorite detail: the beam tubes were insulated when they went in, but after years of operating at Livingston, the insulation started failing. Turned out rodents had been chewing through it, and crews had to go down the 4km arms pulling the insulation out by hand. An instrument that can measure changes smaller than a fraction of a proton, taken down by rats. Every time I join a tour of the site, I learn and notice new details I hadn't before.
