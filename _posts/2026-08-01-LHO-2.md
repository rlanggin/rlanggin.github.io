# Two Months In
It's hard to comprehend two months have flown by since I first arrived at LHO in June. In that time, I've attended a regional conference, presented at collaboration meetings, gone on a public tour with legendary Fred Raab, explored more of southeastern Washington, and licked the concrete encasing the X-arm beam tube. That last one has been a joke back home since before I even got here, but little did everyone know I was very serious about this task.

## HAM7 Day

One of my favorite days this summer was getting hands-on in the LVEA with HAM7 (Horizontal Access Module 7), which houses the quantum squeezer and its crystal — it converts one higher-energy photon into two lower-energy ones to produce squeezed light, giving us more sensitivity at higher frequencies and letting us beat the quantum noise limit. [More on the squeezer here](https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/news/ligo20231023).

Working in-chamber meant full "bunny suit" garb to keep our human contamination away from the detector. Once suited up, I assisted staff scientist Camilla Compton with aligning laser beams — laser alignment cards and my iPhone camera doing the work of seeing the near-infrared and green light. Only a couple of hours of adjusting the squeezer paths, but in layers of clean-room garb it felt like a full workout. Worth it to see one of the optics tables up close before the chamber doors closed. Also worth noting: a snake made its way into the clean room at some point this summer, which is not a sentence I expected to type when I signed up for a physics fellowship.

## GWANW: Hosting the GW Explorer Tutorial

A highlight of the last few weeks was the Gravitational Wave Astrophysics in the Northwest (GWANW) meeting, held right here at LHO. I presented my home research on Early Warning GWs and hosted a tutorial built around my GW Explorer project (read about the project: [GW Explorer Workshop at Las Vegas Academy](https://rachellanggin.com/blog/2025/GWExplorer/)). Getting real researchers to actually use the tutorial was the best kind of validation — lots of positive feedback, interest from potential contributors, and even requests to translate it into more languages. Excited to see it grow beyond the Las Vegas bubble it started in.

## A Saturday Tour with Fred Raab

I tagged along on one of the First Saturday Public Tours, led by Fred Raab, who's been with LIGO since the very beginning. Getting the history from someone who was actually there isn't something I take for granted, so I showed up on a Saturday for this one. Fred told us about the installation and what's been learned after 20 years of running. My favorite detail: the beam tubes were insulated when installed, but after years at Livingston the insulation started failing — turned out rodents had been chewing through it, and crews had to walk the 4km arms pulling insulation out by hand. An instrument that can measure changes smaller than a fraction of a proton, nearly taken down by rats. Every tour I join, I notice something new.

Two months in out of my six month stay and this experience has already been so fulfilling. As I am writing this, we just locked the Y-arm of the interferometer after pumping down the corner station for the past month. I'm really excited to be here as we continue commissioning the detector and move into the upcoming Observing Run in November. Thanks for reading and check back in soon for more updates!
